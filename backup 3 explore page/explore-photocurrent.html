The channelrhodopsin-2 photocurrent

Shine blue light on a cell that makes channelrhodopsin-2 and an inward current flows within milliseconds. It peaks, sags to a lower plateau while the light stays on, and switches off within tens of milliseconds when the light goes out. A second flash soon after is smaller: the protein needs seconds in the dark to recover. This page builds that current from a four-state model of the protein and lets you run the protocols yourself.

01 · The canonical trace

One pulse, five numbers

Light on: the current jumps to a peak. Light still on: it sags to a steady level. Light off: the channels close. Every ChR2 paper reports the same five measures of this shape, and the page fits them to the trace as it is recorded.

The detail · the model and the fits+

The protein is treated as four states: two closed (C1 dark-adapted, C2 light-adapted) and two open (O1 with full conductance, O2 with a tenth of it). Light drives C1→O1 and C2→O2. While the light stays on, open channels drift from O1 to O2 and close into C2, which reopens poorly. That drift is the sag from peak to plateau. C2 returns to C1 only slowly, over seconds, which is why the protein needs time in the dark before it gives a full peak again.

dO1/dt = k1·p·C1 − (Gd1 + e12)·O1 + e21·O2
dO2/dt = k2·p·C2 − (Gd2 + e21)·O2 + e12·O1
dC2/dt = Gd2·O2 − (k2·p + Gr)·C2
dp/dt = (S − p)/τChR2, C1 = 1 − O1 − O2 − C2
I = g·(O1 + γ·O2)·V0·(1 − e−(V − E)/V0), γ = 0.1, E = 0 mV

k1 and k2 are proportional to the photon flux absorbed by retinal (cross-section 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ m², scaled by the spectral sensitivity at the chosen wavelength). p is a light-activation variable with τChR2 = 1.3 ms, S = 1 while the light is on. e12 and e21 grow with the log of irradiance; Gd1 and Gr depend on voltage. The rate constants and their light and voltage dependence are those published for ChR2(H134R) by Williams et al. (2013). The rectification uses their exponential constant (V0 = 42.8 mV) with the reversal set at 0 mV.

The equations are integrated by fourth-order Runge–Kutta (25 µs steps with light on or just after it, 0.5 ms in the dark). The current is sampled at 10 kHz, then given 5 pA rms of Gaussian-filtered noise (1 kHz corner) from a fixed seed, so every visitor sees the same recording. The measures are fitted to that noisy record: Ip is the extremum of a 0.5 ms running mean; τON is a single exponential from light onset to the peak; τINACT from the peak to the end of the pulse (pulses of 20 ms or longer); ISS is the mean over the last 20 ms of the pulse (pulses of 100 ms or longer); τOFF from 0.3 ms after light off to the end of the record.

Sources · Williams et al. 2013 [9] · Nikolic et al. 2009 [5] · Hegemann et al. 2005 [2]

02 · Irradiance

More light: bigger, faster, then saturated

Brighter light opens channels faster and drives them into the desensitised state faster. The peak grows and then levels off. Closing, which happens after the light is off, hardly changes.

The detail+

The light-driven steps, C1→O1 and C2→O2, are proportional to photon flux, so opening and the approach to the plateau both speed up as irradiance rises. The peak saturates because a cell has a fixed number of channels: once light opens them faster than they close, more light adds little. The transition from O1 to O2 also grows with irradiance, which is why the plateau does not rise in proportion to the peak.

τOFF is measured after the light is switched off. It is set by the dark closing rates, Gd1 and Gd2, so it barely depends on the irradiance used during the pulse. It does depend on voltage (section 03).

Sources · Williams et al. 2013 [9] · Mattis et al. 2011 [8]

03 · Current and voltage

Reversal at zero, and strong inward rectification

ChR2 lets several kinds of cation through (protons, sodium, potassium, some calcium), so its current reverses near 0 mV. It carries inward current far more readily than outward current.

The detail+

The rectification is empirical: an exponential driving-force term with a 42.8 mV constant, which makes the current at −80 mV several times larger than at +40 mV. Voltage also changes the kinetics. In the published parameter set the O1→C1 closing rate, Gd1, slows at depolarised potentials and the C2→C1 recovery rate, Gr, slows too. Hold at +20 mV and τOFF on the bench above lengthens accordingly.

Because the plateau is not a fixed fraction of the peak at every voltage, an I–V measured from the plateau and one measured from the peak are different curves. Papers that report "the" ChR2 I–V usually mean the peak.

Sources · Nagel et al. 2003 [1] · Williams et al. 2013 [9]

04 · Desensitisation and recovery

Why a second flash is smaller

After a long pulse, many channels sit in the light-adapted closed state and take seconds in darkness to return. Flash again too soon and the second peak is smaller. Drag the dark interval to see how long the protein needs.

How to read & use this+

Use. Drag the dark interval to set the time between two identical 500 ms pulses, S1 and S2.

Read the left plot. S2 (gold) drawn on top of S1 (grey), both aligned to light onset. The gap between the two peaks is how much the protein has not yet recovered.

Read the right plot. The S2 peak as a fraction of S1 against the dark interval. The dashed curve is the exponential fit; its time constant is τR in the tiles. A value of 1.0 means full recovery.

The detail+

At the end of S1 the open channels have closed within tens of milliseconds, but a large fraction of the protein is left in C2. C2 opens only into the low-conductance O2 state, so channels in C2 contribute little to the S2 peak. The S2 peak recovers as C2 empties back into C1 at the rate Gr, which in the published set is a fraction of a per-second and voltage dependent.

For experiments this sets a practical limit. Repeated pulses at short intervals give a smaller, plateau-sized response after the first. Either space them by the recovery time or treat the first response as different from the rest.

Sources · Lin et al. 2009 [4] · Williams et al. 2013 [9]

05 · Colour and temperature

Blue works best; warm runs faster

ChR2 responds most strongly to blue light near 470 nm. At body temperature every step of the cycle runs faster, and a larger share of the current is sustained through the pulse.

The detail+

The spectral sensitivity is an asymmetric bell centred on 470 nm, broader on the blue side, approximated here to the published action spectrum. It scales the light-driven rates only. The plot uses equal photon flux at every wavelength; at equal power, longer wavelengths deliver slightly more photons.

Temperature scales each rate by its own Q10, referenced to 22 °C: light-driven steps 1.46, Gd1 1.97, Gd2 1.77, e12 1.46, e21 2.77, Gr 2.56. Because the steps that refill the open state (e21, Gr) are more temperature sensitive than those that empty it, the plateau is a larger fraction of the peak when warm. Treat these Q10 values as approximate.

Methods note

What this model is, and what it is not

The published, fully parameterised four-state set is for ChR2(H134R) expressed in HEK cells (Williams et al. 2013), fitted to five holding voltages, four irradiances and a sixty-condition recovery protocol. That set is the default here. The wild-type and ChETA options keep the same scheme with rates rescaled by us towards published off-kinetics and desensitisation; their captions say so.

Currents are normalised so that H134R at −60 mV, 22 °C and 5.5 mW/mm² of 470 nm light peaks at −1 nA. Real amplitudes depend on expression level and cell size. The spectral shape and the Q10 values are approximations stated in sections 05.

These are simulations of a published model, computed in your browser. They are for explanation. Experimental data appear in the papers cited below.
