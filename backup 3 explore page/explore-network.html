From one photon to a network

A photon bends one bond in retinal. The protein around it opens a pore that carries a few femtoamps. A hundred thousand such pores carry nanoamps, enough to fire a neuron, and a few hundred neurons firing together move a network. Each scene below adds one step in scale and keeps the recording beside the explanation.

01 · The protein in the membrane

Seven helices, one retinal, three gates

ChR2 crosses the membrane seven times. Retinal, the light-absorbing molecule, is bonded to a lysine on the seventh helix. The pore runs between helices 1, 2, 3 and 7 and is closed in the dark at three places along its length.

Structure
PDB 6EID: wild-type ChR2 crystallised as a dimer. Each protomer has its own retinal and its own pore.
Retinal
All-trans retinal joined to K257 on TM7 through a protonated Schiff base, in a pocket between TM3 and TM7.
Gates
Extracellular, central and intracellular. The central gate holds E90 on TM2. At the intracellular gate the side chains of H134 and R268 block the pore.
What is missing
No structure of the open channel has been solved. The open state in the next scene is a molecular-dynamics model.

02 · Blue light, and the photocycle

One bond twists, and the pore fills with water

After a 470 nm photon is absorbed, the protein passes through a series of intermediates, each named after the colour it absorbs. Step through them to see when the gates open and when ions can pass.

How to read & use this+

Use. Click a state name to jump to it, or use Previous and Next to step through the cycle. Play runs through it on its own.

Read the ring. The photocycle drawn as a loop. The highlighted state is the one shown on the right; the arrows show the order and the light-driven step.

Read the right panel. The same cut through the protein as in 01, now in the selected state: which gates are open and whether water and ions reach the pore. The text and tiles give the state's name, whether it conducts, and its approximate lifetime.

The detail · from photocycle to the four-state model+

In the dark state a hydrogen-bond network joins K93 and E90 on TM2, E123 on TM3 and D253 on TM7, and water cannot reach the central gate. In the simulations E90 loses its proton as P390-early turns into P390-late, which breaks the link between TM2 and TM7. The number of waters at the central gate rises in steps: low in P390-early, about 70% of the open value in P390-late, and the full value in P520. The free energy for a Na⁺ ion crossing the central gate of P520 is almost flat.

The whole-cell model used in Module 1 and in the scenes below compresses this cycle into four states. D470 corresponds to C1, the conducting P520 to O1, and the desensitised P480 to C2, which opens only weakly (O2) and returns to the dark state over seconds. The model reproduces the current; it does not resolve the intermediates.

03 · Microscopic to macroscopic

A channel too small to record

One open ChR2 channel conducts 40–90 femtosiemens, about 0.005 pA at −60 mV. The quietest patch recording has noise of around 0.1 pA, twenty times larger. So single ChR2 openings are not recorded directly. Add channels and the current climbs out of the noise; at a million it is the nanoamp photocurrent of Module 1.

The detail+

Each channel follows the same four-state scheme as Module 1, but as a random process: in every 50 µs step, the number of channels leaving each state is drawn from a binomial distribution with the transition probabilities of that step. With one channel you see single openings to O1 and the smaller O2 level; with many, the fluctuations average out and the sum converges on N·i·Po(t), where Po is the open-equivalent fraction (O1 + 0.1·O2) of the deterministic model.

The single-channel conductance of ChR2 has been estimated from noise analysis rather than seen directly; published values fall between 40 and 90 fS. The page uses −0.005 pA for a full O1 opening at −60 mV. Background noise is 0.1 pA rms filtered at 1 kHz, close to the floor of a good single-channel patch. For comparison, the lab records muscle acetylcholine receptor openings of several picoamps, a thousand times larger.

04 · The action potential

A flash of light, a spike within milliseconds

Put the photocurrent into a neuron and a brief pulse of blue light fires a single spike a few milliseconds later. A dimmer or shorter pulse depolarises the membrane without firing it. The threshold belongs to the neuron: a shorter pulse needs brighter light.

The curve is the lowest irradiance that fires one spike, found by bisection for each pulse length. Above it, every point fires; below it, none does. It flattens for long pulses at the light level that produces the neuron's rheobase current, and rises steeply for pulses shorter than the membrane can integrate.

Boyden and colleagues showed in 2005 that millisecond pulses of blue light on ChR2-expressing hippocampal neurons fire spikes with millisecond precision. This scene reproduces that result in the Hodgkin–Huxley membrane used on the Teaching page and in HHsim.

Use. Pick a variant and set the irradiance and pulse length. The neuron is simulated again on every change.

Read the upper plot. Membrane voltage in mV. A spike is a fast rise past 0 mV and back; without one, the light only bumps the voltage up. The lower trace is the ChR2 current during the same pulse, with the coloured bar showing when the light is on.

Read the strength–duration plot. Open circles are the threshold irradiance for each pulse length. Your current settings are the filled blue dot: above the curve fires a spike, below it does not.

The detail+

The membrane is the Hodgkin–Huxley squid axon (resting near −65 mV), with its gating rates scaled from 6.3 °C to 12 °C using Q10 = 3 so that it can follow the pulse trains in the next scene. The ChR2 current is added as a membrane current, IChR2 = g·(O1 + 0.1·O2)·G(V), with the photocycle integrated together with V, m, h and n by fourth-order Runge–Kutta at 10 µs. Because the closing rate Gd1 and the rectification depend on voltage, the channel feels the spike it causes.

The size of the ChR2 conductance relative to the membrane (25 µA/cm² for the normalised −1 nA peak) is ours. It sets where the strength–duration curve sits on the irradiance axis, not its shape. A spike is counted when V crosses 0 mV; latency is measured from light onset to that crossing.

05 · Pulse trains, and why kinetics matter

Following the light, then falling behind

Twenty 2 ms pulses at a fixed rate. At low rates every pulse fires a spike. As the rate rises, each pulse arrives before the protein has recovered from the last one, the current per pulse shrinks, and spikes drop out. ChETA, with faster kinetics, keeps up for longer.

Modulating, not driving

Most experiments do not impose every spike. Here the same neuron fires irregularly on a noisy input, and dim continuous light is added for half a second in eight trials. The rate goes up and the spike times shift; no spike is dictated by the light.

The detail+

To compare kinetics rather than current size, each variant is driven at three times its own 2 ms single-spike threshold. Fidelity is the fraction of pulses followed by at least one spike within the shorter of one period and 15 ms; extra spikes are any others. With the rescaled rates used here, wild-type ChR2 desensitises more during a train than H134R, so its per-pulse current falls and spikes are lost first; ChETA closes about three times faster and loses less current between pulses. At the highest rates the neuron's own refractory period also limits following.

The wild-type and ChETA rates are the H134R set rescaled by us towards published off-kinetics and desensitisation (Module 1, methods note). The ranking follows Gunaydin et al. 2010; the exact rates at which each variant fails depend on the neuron and should not be read as measured values. The modulation panel uses a bias current of 3 µA/cm² plus Ornstein–Uhlenbeck noise (σ = 1.5 µA/cm², τ = 5 ms), with a fixed seed per trial.

06 · The network

Choose who sees the light

Four hundred neurons, 80% excitatory and 20% inhibitory, sparsely connected and firing irregularly. Genetic targeting decides which cells make ChR2. Light the excitatory cells and the network follows the pulses; light the inhibitory cells and the whole network goes quiet.

Try. Switch ChR2 from excitatory to inhibitory cells with the same light and watch the excitatory rate fall.

The detail+

Cells are conductance-based leaky integrate-and-fire units (τm 20 ms, threshold −50 mV, reset −60 mV, refractory 5 ms for excitatory and 2 ms for inhibitory cells) with exponential excitatory (5 ms, 0 mV) and inhibitory (10 ms, −80 mV) synapses, after the benchmark networks of Vogels & Abbott 2005 and Brette et al. 2007. Each cell receives 32 excitatory and 8 inhibitory recurrent inputs chosen at random, plus independent Poisson background input that holds the network in an asynchronous, irregular state. Integration is forward Euler at 0.1 ms.

Expressing cells receive the H134R photocurrent computed once at −60 mV for each light level, rescaled by each cell's driving force with the same inward rectification. With "Scattered", each cell is given a random depth and the irradiance it receives falls exponentially to 5% at the deepest cells, so cells near the fibre are driven harder than cells far from it. "Locked" is the fraction of excitatory spikes during the light that fall within 10 ms of a pulse onset; the chance level is that window as a fraction of the period. The network is generic and is not a model of a particular brain area.

Sources · Brunel 2000 [12] · Vogels & Abbott 2005 [13] · Brette et al. 2007 [14]

Methods note

What is measured, what is modelled, what is ours

Scenes 01 and 02 are schematics. The dark-state layout follows the crystal structure 6EID; the intermediates follow a published molecular-dynamics study, which is a model of the open channel, not a structure of it.

Scenes 03 to 06 are computed in your browser from the four-state ChR2 model of Module 1 (Williams et al. 2013, H134R; other variants rescaled by us). The Hodgkin–Huxley equations are the published ones with temperature scaling; the network follows standard benchmark parameters. The coupling strengths between the photocurrent and each cell, the light attenuation and the background input are our choices, stated in each detail block. Every number printed beside a figure is measured from the simulated trace.

These are simulations, for explanation. Experimental data appear in the papers cited below.
